Counting bacteria only works if the plate you count is trustworthy — and most real samples are far too concentrated to plate directly. Serial dilution is how labs bring a sample down into a countable range, and the same handful of formulas underneath it show up everywhere a bacterial count decides something important:

Medicine — is an infection clearing with treatment?
Food safety — is this batch within a safe microbial load?
Environment — is the water safe to swim in today?
01

How a dilution actually works

Before building a series, get the core idea: you're mixing a small volume of sample — containing an unknown number of live bacteria — into a larger volume of diluent (e.g. sterile saline, water, or broth). Each step reduces the concentration, or bacterial number expressed as CFU/mL (Colony-Forming Units per volume), by a fixed, predictable factor.

One tube. Add 1 mL of sample to 9 mL of diluent and you have 10 mL total, with the original bugs spread through 10× the volume — a 1:10 dilution (10⁻¹).

dilution factor = sample volume ÷ (sample + diluent)
1 mL ÷ (1 mL + 9 mL) = 1/10

Chain it — that's "serial." Take 1 mL out of that tube and repeat into a fresh 9 mL blank, and you multiply factors: 1/10 × 1/10 = 1:100 (10⁻²). One more identical step gives 1:1,000 (10⁻³), and so on — always in clean powers of ten.

total dilution = previous tube's dilution × next tube's own factor

Each additional tube in the chain buys you one more order of magnitude of dilution — which is exactly what's needed to bring an impossibly dense sample down into a plate you can actually count.

03

From dilution tube to plate

A dilution tube is invisible until a measured volume is grown out on agar. There are two standard ways to get it there — and they change how and where the colonies end up.

Pour plate

1Pipette the diluted sample straight into an empty sterile dish.
2Pour in agar cooled to ~45–50°C and swirl to mix.
3Let it solidify — cells are now locked throughout the agar, not just on top.
Good for handling denser samples; colonies grow in 3D, so some form small and embedded. The warm agar can stress heat-sensitive organisms.

Spread plate

1Pipette a small volume (often 0.1 mL) onto a dish of already-solid agar.
2Drag a sterile spreader (or sterile beads) across the surface until evenly distributed.
3Cells sit only on the surface, exposed to air.
Colonies stay separate and easy to pick for further testing. Volume is limited so the liquid doesn't run — usually a smaller fraction of the tube.
Either way, the plate is incubated (commonly ~24–48 h at 35–37°C for many bacteria) before the colonies are counted — which is exactly what the next steps below do.

07

Quick reference

Dilution factor / tube

amount of sample ÷ (amount of sample + amount of diluent)

Total dilution

previous tube's total dilution × next tube's own dilution factor

Standard formula

colony count ÷ (total dilution of that tube × amount plated)

Countable window

30–300 colonies per plate. Below 30: too little to be reliable. Above 300: TNTC / overlapping.

08

So — what exactly is a colony?

One last idea ties the whole worksheet together: why counting colonies is a way of counting cells.

A colony is a visible, dome-shaped mound of bacteria on a plate — but it doesn't start that way. It starts as a single bacterial cell that landed on (or in) the agar, found nutrients, and began dividing by binary fission: one cell becomes two, two become four, four become eight...

Doubling every 20–30 minutes for many species, that single cell needs roughly 25–30 generations — around a day of growth for many bacteria — to build a mound of tens of millions to billions of cells, large enough to see with the naked eye. Every colony on a plate, however different in size, traces back to just one founding cell (or occasionally a small clump of cells that landed together).

That's exactly why colony counts are reported in CFU — Colony-Forming Units — each colony represents one original viable cell (or clump) capable of forming a colony, not the trillions of cells inside it. Count the colonies, and you're counting how many original live cells were in your plated volume.
