What PCR does

The polymerase chain reaction copies one chosen stretch of DNA, defined by two primers, over and over. Each cycle roughly doubles the number of copies, so after 30 cycles a few molecules become billions: enough to see on a gel or to detect with a fluorescent dye.

A microbiology story
Taq polymerase comes from Thermus aquaticus, a thermophilic bacterium isolated from a Yellowstone hot spring (Brock, 1969). Its heat stability made automated PCR possible (Mullis, 1983).
Exponential growth
Copies = N₀ × (1 + E)ⁿ, where E is the efficiency (ideal 1.0 = perfect doubling). Growth slows into a plateau as primers and dNTPs run out and the polymerase tires.
Specificity
Primers set where copying starts and stops. The annealing temperature, Mg²⁺ and primer design decide whether only the target is amplified.

Controls you always run

  • Positive control: known target. Proves the reaction can work.
  • No-template control (NTC): water instead of sample. Must stay negative; a target band means contamination.
  • Internal/extraction control: a spiked or housekeeping target in every sample. If it fails, the sample may contain inhibitors (heme, heparin, bile salts…), so a negative result is invalid.
  • Ladder on the gel to confirm product size.

Contamination control

  • Aerosol-barrier (filter) tips.
  • Separate pre-PCR (clean) and post-PCR (amplified product) areas, with one-way workflow.
  • Never open amplified tubes in the clean area: a single tube holds ~10¹² copies.
  • dUTP/UNG systems destroy carry-over products in many clinical assays.

Primer Design Challenge

The template below is the top strand (5'→3') of a target region. Choose one forward and one reverse primer, each written 5'→3'. Good primers are 18–25 nt long with 40–60% GC, Tm within ~5 °C of each other, a G or C at the 3' end ("GC clamp"), no strong self-complementarity, and 3' ends that do not pair with each other. The reverse primer must match the bottom strand, so its sequence is the reverse complement of the top strand.

How to use: PCR Training Lab

In brief: Build a PCR master mix, program the thermocycler, design primers, read real-time qPCR curves, and troubleshoot failed runs. Then send your reactions to the gel.

PCR Bench: step by step

  1. Choose an assay (mecA, stx2, tcdB or 16S rRNA).
  2. Master mix: type the µL of each reagent for a 25 µL reaction. Final concentrations update live; tick Show target ranges if you need a hint.
  3. Hygiene: tick both good-practice boxes, or the no-template control will be contaminated.
  4. Program the cycler: set denaturation, annealing (≈ primer Tm − 3 to 5 °C), extension time (~1 min per kb) and cycles.
  5. Run: watch the copy-number curve, then read the run report and the result for each tube.
  6. Send to gel: opens the Gel Electrophoresis Lab with your tubes loaded.

Other tabs

  • Primer Design: pick one forward and one reverse primer, then press Evaluate this pair. Find the best-designed pair.
  • Real-time qPCR: drag the threshold, click legend chips to show or hide curves, read Ct values and the standard curve, then answer the questions.
  • Troubleshooting: diagnose six failed gels.
  • Quiz: 12 questions.
