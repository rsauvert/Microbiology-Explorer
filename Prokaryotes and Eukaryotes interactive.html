🦠 + 🧬 What if a bacterium swallowed a mitochondrion and kept it?

Click to reveal the ancient plot twist…

Congratulations — you just rediscovered endosymbiotic theory.
That’s basically how eukaryotes got mitochondria ~1.5 billion years ago. A prokaryote (alphaproteobacterium) was engulfed by an ancestral host cell and never left. Same story with chloroplasts (from cyanobacteria). You monster. You beautiful, textbook monster.

What if humans had 70S ribosomes instead of 80S?

Click at your own risk…

Most antibiotics would also kill you.
Aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, macrolides, chloramphenicol, and linezolid all target 70S ribosomes. The reason they (usually) don’t kill us is that our cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S. Our mitochondria still have 70S ribosomes — which is why some antibiotics have mitochondrial toxicity. Biology is metal.

What if E. coli decided to grow a nucleus tomorrow?

Click for the bureaucratic nightmare…

It would stop being a prokaryote by definition… and also probably explode.
Building a nuclear envelope, linear chromosomes, mitosis machinery, and proper organelles is not a weekend project. Evolution took over a billion years. Also, transcription and translation would no longer be coupled — a major lifestyle change for a bacterium that prides itself on speed.

What if eukaryotes had peptidoglycan cell walls?

Click to meet your new rigid self…

You’d be a lot harder to hug… and lysozyme would be a bioweapon.
Animal cells deliberately lack rigid walls so they can move, change shape, and form tissues. Plants use cellulose; fungi use chitin. If we had peptidoglycan, every time someone cried (lysozyme in tears) or ate an egg white, cellular chaos could ensue. Also β-lactam antibiotics would become personal attacks.

Who would win in a fight: one elephant or one trillion bacteria?

The ultimate matchup…

Technically the bacteria already live on and inside the elephant, so they’ve already infiltrated.
Biomass-wise, bacteria on Earth outweigh all animals combined. Numerically, your own body has roughly as many bacterial cells as human cells. The “fight” was over before it started. Prokaryotes win by sheer existential ubiquity. Eukaryotes win by writing the history books (and this game).

Can a prokaryote ever become a multicellular organism?

Click for the plot of several origin-of-complexity papers…

They already do… kind of.
Biofilms, cyanobacterial filaments, myxobacteria fruiting bodies, and Streptomyces hyphae show coordinated multicellular behavior. True complex multicellularity with specialized tissues is rare outside eukaryotes, but prokaryotes are not strictly “loners.” They just prefer the soft launch of cooperation over the full nuclear upgrade.

Deep Lore (Actually Useful)

Prokaryote Power-Ups

  • No nucleus = transcription & translation can happen at the same time (speedrun mode)
  • Plasmids let them trade antibiotic resistance like rare Pokémon cards
  • Endospores = “pause the game and survive the apocalypse”
  • Horizontal gene transfer makes their evolution feel like multiplayer
  • Archaea invented weird lipids and live in places that would melt your face

Eukaryote Flexes

  • Nucleus = protected genome + sophisticated gene regulation
  • Mitochondria = efficient ATP factories (thanks, ancient bacteria)
  • Cytoskeleton = shape-shifting, phagocytosis, and mitosis
  • Sexual reproduction = genetic shuffling for long-term survival
  • Multicellularity with true tissues and organs (the ultimate cooperative DLC)

The Shared Origin Story

All life shares a common ancestor. Eukaryotes are essentially the result of a successful long-term merger between prokaryotic lineages (endosymbiosis). Your mitochondria still have their own circular DNA and 70S ribosomes — little prokaryotic ghosts living rent-free inside you.

Without prokaryotes there would be no eukaryotes, no oxygen atmosphere as we know it, and no one to make this ridiculous game.
